The dictator game

  • The game you have just played is known as the dictator game (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler 1986).

  • It is designed to study decisions in situations where self-interest and equality are in conflict.

  • Do you think most people would behave as you did in this situation?
    • For example, how different do you think your behavior would be from that of an inmate?
  • Birkeland et al. (2014) conducted an experiment to answer this question.

Social preferences

  • What have we learned from dictator and public-goods games?
    • Many people behave altruistically: they are willing to sacrifice some of their own payoff to increase someone else’s.
    • People often repay kindness with kindness and punish behavior they consider unfair.
  • Altruism and reciprocity are examples of social preferences.

Social preferences (cont’d)

Social preferences

Preferences that place value on what happens to other people.

  • People differ greatly in their willingness to give and to reciprocate altruistic behavior. Some behave prosocially, while others tend to free-ride.

Altruistic behavior: stylized facts

Why do people give?

  • The literature suggests at least two motives.

  • Pure altruism: I help others because I want them to be better off.

  • A pure altruist is motivated solely by other people’s well-being, not by personal gain.

    • What matters is the donation’s ultimate effect on the person receiving help.

Why do people give? (cont’d)

  • Impure altruism (Andreoni 1990): I help others because…
    • I enjoy the personal satisfaction of doing good — the warm glow of giving.
    • I worry that not helping might expose me to social disapproval.
  • An impure altruist may give more when other people can observe the donation.
    • Giving can be a way to signal something positive about oneself.

Why do people give? (cont’d)

  • Suppose you are deciding whether to help fund a public library.

  • If you are a pure altruist, you donate because you value knowing that the library will be built.

  • If you are an impure altruist, you donate because you value knowing that you contributed to the project.

    • Perhaps what appeals to you is seeing your name on a plaque that publicly recognizes what you did for the community.

  • Pure and impure altruism are not mutually exclusive.

Why do people give? Experimental evidence

  • To investigate the roles of pure and impure altruism in charitable giving, Harbaugh, Mayr, and Burghart (2007) used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to scan people playing a modified dictator game.

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

  • Each participant received $100.

  • Some participants could choose whether to donate part of this amount to a local food bank.

  • For the other participants, money was deducted automatically from their endowment, much like an income tax, and transferred to the food bank.

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

  • If people were motivated only by impure altruism, then…
    • They should derive satisfaction from giving voluntarily.
    • They should derive no satisfaction from compulsory transfers.

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

  • If people were motivated by pure altruism, then…
    • They should also derive satisfaction from compulsory transfers.
    • Pure altruists care only about how much money the food bank receives, not how it gets there.

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

  • Satisfaction…
    • Increases as transfers to the food bank increase and participants’ own losses decrease.
    • Is higher when transfers are voluntary (solid lines) rather than compulsory (dashed lines).

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

Why do people give? Experimental evidence (cont’d)

  • Consistent with pure altruism, compulsory transfers to a charity generate satisfaction and activate brain regions involved in reward processing.

  • Consistent with the warm glow motive, satisfaction and neural activity increase further when people make voluntary transfers.

  • Both pure and impure altruism play a role in charitable giving.

A tale of day-care centers

  • Social preferences can be highly sensitive to the context in which decisions are made. Consider the following example.

  • Parents sometimes struggle to pick up their children from day care on time.

  • When parents arrive late, day-care staff have to stay beyond their scheduled hours.

  • What would you do to discourage parents from arriving late?

A tale of day-care centers (cont’d)

  • Gneezy and Rustichini (2000) conducted a field experiment in Haifa. They introduced fines for late pickup at some day-care centers, while leaving others unchanged as a control group.

  • The following notice was posted at the centers in the treatment group:

NOTICE TO PARENTS

Because some parents have been arriving late, we have decided — with the approval of the Israel Private Daycare Authority — to fine parents who pick up their children late. Beginning next Sunday, a fine of NIS 10 [about €3] will be charged whenever a child is collected after 4:10 p.m.

A tale of day-care centers (cont’d)

  • Why did the fines have the opposite of their intended effect?

  • Possible explanation

    • Before the fines were introduced, most parents arrived on time because they felt a moral obligation not to keep the staff at work late.
    • In other words, parents behaved altruistically toward the staff.
    • The fines signaled that late pickup had a price and could therefore be bought, just like groceries or shoes.

A tale of day-care centers (cont’d)

  • Possible explanation (cont’d)
    • By putting a price on late pickup, the fines appear to have weakened parents’ sense of moral obligation toward the staff.
    • The incentive sent the wrong message: “It is fine to be late as long as you pay.”

Crowding out social preferences

  • The experiment suggests that material incentives (such as the price of being late) can sometimes make people behave more selfishly than they otherwise would.

  • When fines or prices have this unintended effect, we say that incentives appealing to material self-interest crowd out social preferences.

  • This crowding-out effect can persist. In this example, parents continued to pick up their children late even after the fines were removed.
